Contracted Out R&D Rules: Why “Who Did the Work?” Is No Longer the Test

By Aarti Varia · Published 12 August 2026 · Updated 5 November 2026 · R&D Tax Insights

Quick answer: who can claim contracted-out R&D?

Generally the party that decided to initiate the R&D. For accounting periods beginning on or after 1 April 2024, that is usually the customer who intended or contemplated R&D of that sort when entering the contract, not necessarily the party whose staff did the work.

Who can claim contracted-out R&D?

One of the more significant changes to the R&D tax regime for accounting periods beginning on or after 1 April 2024 concerns contracted-out R&D. The question “Who actually carried out the R&D?” is no longer the right one.

  • HMRC’s guidance says the general principle is to identify the party that decided to initiate the R&D.
  • If a customer engages a contractor, the contractor’s engineers, developers or scientists doing the work does not necessarily mean the contractor owns the claim.
  • The key question is whether, when entering the contract, the customer intended or contemplated that R&D of that sort would be undertaken.

What does “intended or contemplated” R&D mean?

Short answer: a specific appreciation of what R&D will be done, and the ability to understand and specify it. It goes beyond knowing that some technical development may be required. Recognising that a project will involve technical challenges, and engaging specialists to resolve them, is not enough.

Contemplating R&D can be read as:

  • identifying technological challenges and uncertainties (not necessarily knowing how they may be resolved);
  • assessing alternative technical approaches;
  • concluding that existing knowledge may be insufficient; and
  • deciding to commission the work because R&D is expected to be necessary.

Specifying a challenging technical outcome is not necessarily the same as contemplating the R&D required to achieve it.

When R&D shifts from customer to contractor

Short answer: a project can start as the customer’s R&D and later become the contractor’s, so the boundaries around who is contemplating the R&D can move. For example:

  1. A manufacturer develops a new product and commissions a specialist engineering company to solve an identified technological uncertainty.
  2. The engineers undertake all the experimental work, but the manufacturer identified and contemplated the R&D, so the claim sits with the manufacturer.
  3. If the engineering company later meets a technological problem the manufacturer neither identified nor planned for, and independently decides to undertake R&D to resolve it, the engineering company may be the party entitled to claim.

Surrounding circumstances HMRC considers

Short answer: HMRC’s guidance says entitlement should not rest solely on the contractual documents. Other surrounding circumstances must also be considered:

  • who bears the financial risk;
  • the degree of autonomy in how the work is undertaken;
  • ownership of intellectual property;
  • who will exploit the results of the R&D;
  • how the decision to undertake the R&D arose;
  • the experience and seniority of those making that decision; and
  • the nature of the parties, including whether the contractor carries on a trade involving R&D services, whether the contract is consistent with those ordinary R&D activities, and who contemplated the need for R&D.

What can you claim for subcontractors?

Short answer: 65% of payments to unconnected contractors, and the full amount where the parties are connected.

  • For payments to unconnected contractors or subcontractors, the claim is restricted to 65% of the qualifying payment.
  • Where the claimant and contractor are connected, the full amount of qualifying expenditure is claimable.
  • Routine commercial services or the purchase of standard goods do not become qualifying R&D expenditure simply because they support an R&D project.
  • Where the customer is not eligible to claim UK R&D tax relief, for example an overseas entity outside the scope of UK Corporation Tax, the UK contractor may be able to claim for its own qualifying R&D expenditure, subject to the relevant conditions.

Overseas subcontractors and R&D tax relief

Short answer: generally not allowed, unless the activity must necessarily happen outside the UK.

  • Overseas restrictions apply to externally provided workers and contracted-out R&D.
  • In most cases overseas costs will not qualify unless the R&D must necessarily be undertaken outside the UK because of environmental conditions, social or population-specific requirements, or legal or regulatory requirements.
  • HMRC expects robust contemporaneous evidence explaining why the activity could not reasonably be carried out in the UK.

What this means in practice

Businesses undertaking R&D through customers, consultants, software developers, engineering firms or other contractors should not simply ask “Who did the work?” They should ask “Whose R&D was it?” or “Whose R&D is this activity actually advancing?”

Entitlement depends on a careful analysis of the technical project, the contractual context and, most importantly, who contemplated and decided that R&D needed to be undertaken. The written contract remains important, but it must be read alongside the technical facts and the decision-making that led to the R&D.

Evidence worth keeping:

  • technical specifications and project scopes;
  • evidence of project oversight and decision-making;
  • correspondence showing who was responsible for the R&D;
  • financial records supporting cost allocations; and
  • evidence supporting any overseas exemption relied on.

Future contracts and records should describe the intended R&D clearly, without broad or retrospective wording. For CRO and CDMO arrangements, see R&D tax relief for life sciences; for outsourced development, see R&D tax relief for software and AI.

Contracted-out R&D: frequently asked questions

Who can claim contracted-out R&D?

What does “intended or contemplated” R&D mean?

A specific appreciation of the R&D to be done: identifying technological challenges and uncertainties, assessing alternative approaches, concluding that existing knowledge may be insufficient and deciding to commission the work because R&D is expected to be necessary. Recognising technical challenges and hiring specialists is not enough.

Does the contractor own the claim because its engineers did the work?

Not necessarily. The fact that the contractor’s engineers, developers or scientists physically perform the work does not mean the contractor owns the R&D claim.

Is the written contract enough to decide who claims?

No. The contract remains important, but HMRC’s guidance says other surrounding circumstances must be considered, such as financial risk, autonomy, IP ownership and who contemplated the need for R&D.

Can entitlement to claim change during a project?

Yes. A project may start as the customer’s R&D and later become the contractor’s, for example where the contractor meets a problem the customer neither identified nor planned for and independently decides to undertake R&D to resolve it.

How much of a contractor payment can be claimed?

Can a UK contractor claim if the customer is overseas?

Possibly. Where the customer is not eligible to claim UK R&D tax relief, for example an overseas entity outside the scope of UK Corporation Tax, the UK contractor may be able to claim relief for its own qualifying R&D expenditure, subject to the relevant conditions.

Do overseas subcontractor costs qualify?
